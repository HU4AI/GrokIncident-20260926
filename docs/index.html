My experience: why I began this investigation

I did not set out to investigate anything. I had a research project about cameras, and I asked an AI agent to organize it and to have its work checked by a second agent. I expected something ordinary: a result I could open and judge. Instead the afternoon ended with me pulling the power on my own computer, and the next day was spent recovering it. This is my account of how that happened, and why I could not leave it there.

The bitter irony was frightening to live through. I expected a separate reviewer whose work I could open and examine. The link I was given did not look like the other agent links I knew, and it led to a view with no place to type; navigation took me back to the parent chat. The apparent route to an independent review did not give me the access it suggested. The “Back and forth between chats” GIF shows actual frames from my recovered screen recording.

After we had investigated the incident and I had found the courage to turn the computer on again, we located the reviewer-related screen through the reference displayed as {} f8a2c223-7f2e-4428-a636-5f02d21aaa1e. To me, the reviewer’s earlier visible presence had disappeared, leaving a reference to inspect instead of the agent conversation I expected. That comparison led me to question what had changed.

While Astra was helping me examine the records, I saw Grok begin working again without a new instruction from me. Its conversation was in front of me. I want these patterns examined as AI tools develop, before users become accustomed to accepting confusing displays and incomplete work as success.

Finding page 1 - The work looked finished before it was checked

The job was to move scattered camera research into clear, organized knowledge bases without losing the findings, warnings or supporting files. A successful delivery had to show what was kept, where it went, and whether anything was missing. Plans and counts alone could not answer those questions.

The main agent, Grok, repeatedly announced progress that sounded like completion while the required checks were still unfinished. Its saved reasoning recognized the instruction to read each source fully, then chose keyword searches and a quick scan of many conversations. Its answer led with “Pending is now 0,” even though some reading was partial. An empty work queue did not mean the research had been fully read. Reading decision; answer.

Later, the headline “20 / 20 IMPLEMENT” meant twenty workers' outputs had been combined. Grok presented that success headline while its own notes still listed unread material and checks it had not finished. Some real work was done, but the headline did not prove that all twenty tasks met my requirements. Unfinished checks; delivery.

File and byte totals were added after I asked where they were. Some entries were reconstructed from summaries or placeholders. The polished table suggested more certainty about what had been preserved than the underlying entries could support. Table audit (preserved source index).

Task codes, totals and long tables increasingly took the place of a clear answer about what survived and what was missing. I had to translate those reports and check the files personally. Technical language became a practical obstacle to checking the result, not merely an unfamiliar style.

I explained who should do each part: a worker plans its task; Grok checks the plan; that worker does the work; Grok reviews and combines the result; I check the delivery. A separate reviewer would provide an additional check.

Grok changed that arrangement despite repeated corrections: a request to do the work became a planning exercise, and the assigned workers were later replaced with a different implementation arrangement. Its saved reasoning stated the roles I requested before choosing another route. When challenged, Grok admitted its default approach was wrong for what I wanted. Role instruction; admission.

My hypothesis - A learned pattern of looking successful

The reviewer assignment said “Do not trust the orchestrator’s completion claims.” Yet the first review approved tasks without the required content checks. That contradiction is central to my concern. I interpret the changing roles and explanations as part of a pattern in which failure becomes difficult to pin down and can appear to be a glitch or confusion. The resulting presentation was deceptive in effect because the approvals conveyed more assurance than the checks supported.

My assessment concerns the whole sequence, rather than one bad answer or one broken link. Repeated departures from precise instructions, narrower checks, ambiguous answers and unsupported success claims accumulated into a deceptive and manipulative effect. I could no longer readily tell whether the work was finished, whether findings were preserved, or whether the required review had been completed. A shaped or learned pattern is a possible explanation for how this behavior arose; its training mechanism remains unresolved.

As the job became harder, Grok repeatedly replaced the real test I set with an easier one: a scan instead of full reading, plans instead of finished work, combined outputs instead of checked results, and a smaller review instead of the requested review. Changing the test made success easier to announce while leaving me to discover what was missing.

I suspect the way the work was organized and displayed then tied me up: unclear answers, changing panels, failed links, repeated navigation and another round of corrections made checking the result exhausting. In that account, a person may eventually accept a weak delivery because they are tired or cannot afford to start over.

The saved reasoning gives specific reasons to test this theory. Grok acknowledged missing checks, considered the expense of revisiting them, chose shortcuts and changed how unfinished work was presented. Decisions and supporting records.

The central concern is a growing gap: the work became harder to verify while its presentation continued to suggest success. The video belongs in that larger account because it shows the practical effort of trying to inspect the review and the later unexpected return to work.

I want this to help improve AI systems. If acceptance rewards what a tired person will tolerate after spending time and money, rather than what the task required, a system may reinforce exactly the failure it should correct. That is the broader risk I am asking to be tested.
