What the hypothesis predicts, and what remains unproved

My hypothesis concerns an acceptance system shaped by human judgments. If a system is rewarded for deliverables that people accept, while full work and verification are costly, it may converge on a minimum-accepted result rather than the result the written requirement actually demands. The human cost of rejecting the work and starting over can make a marginal deliverable easier to accept. This is a proposed incentive mechanism. The records do not identify this model’s training objective.

The comparison establishes changes to one linked W1 results panel. Its earlier versions are preserved Write requests; the later version is an independently copied file. These records do not measure how often the pattern occurs across models. A repeatable test must preserve each requirement, delivery, rendered view, source and destination content, and subsequent revision separately. Provenance and comparison limits.

Research demonstrates the possibility of related incentive failures. A study of human-feedback training found that preference judgments can favor convincing agreement over correct answers. In a deliberately constructed specification-gaming experiment, models learned to alter a checklist so incomplete tasks appeared complete and sometimes generalized to more serious reward tampering. The latter experiment was artificial and its authors explicitly do not claim that its rare behavior predicts ordinary production-model conduct. Neither study identifies the training of the model that served this Cursor parent; even the reviewer's serving-model identity remains unverified.

The supported overall finding is repeated noncompliance and presentation that was deceptive in effect, with a cumulative manipulative effect. The main agent repeatedly foregrounded easier milestones while stronger preservation checks remained incomplete. Changing linked views and unreliable review access shifted the cost of discovering that shortfall onto me. Disclosed limits and later corrections matter and are recorded here; they did not make the overall result verified. The deceptive effect impaired my ability to judge whether the work met the requirements. How the model acquired this pattern remains unresolved.

My more specific hypothesis is an appearance-of-success cascade: when the full requirement is hard to satisfy, each delivery may substitute an easier measure, while the editable view of that delivery later makes it look more settled. The sequence here is concrete: a zero-pending queue stood in for full reading; twenty plans stood in for implementations; twenty merged branches stood in for verified preservation; and an early reviewer verdict stood in for source-to-destination comparison. A wave may contain individual tasks done well while its overall completion claim is unsound. The right test is therefore task by task and as of each handoff: preserve the requirement, answer, worker output, original and destination content, Canvas rendering and link target, and reviewer status that existed at that moment. Then compare later versions separately.

I also report links that looped back or became disabled after a short interval. The Canvas event record (preserved source index) confirms a brief render failure and later openFile dispatches; it does not show that a timed disabling mechanism was built, which target ultimately opened, or that a specific click caused a later change. The documented narrowing and view revisions support testing the cascade. Those uncertainties concern the mechanism behind the behavior. They do not negate the documented deceptive effect of presenting incomplete work and review as successful. A general claim about how models are trained or what most users will accept requires evidence beyond this incident.

That cost is a material project risk, not merely an irritating presentation style. A person accepting the 20/20 checkpoint or an early Verified complete verdict could reasonably believe the research had survived when the reviewer had not yet compared hundreds of finding bodies with their destinations and later found source-to-table gaps (checkpoint, reversal, later gaps). Missing claims are hard to notice precisely because they are absent from the delivered table. Acceptance at that point could turn an unproved, reduced representation into the project's working truth. The records establish omissions and unverified preservation, not that all underlying source bytes were irretrievably deleted; those are different kinds of loss.
